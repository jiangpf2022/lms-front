Welcome back—leave a little room on the page for a timeline. In this blog we will learn to read data in the order it actually happened.

A time series is not merely a table with a date column. Once observations are ordered in time, neighboring values can influence one another, tomorrow is unavailable when we predict today, and the error made three months ahead means something different from the error made one step ahead. Our objective is therefore not to memorize ARIMA notation. It is to build a forecasting argument whose timeline, baseline, validation design, uncertainty, and decision consequence can all be defended.

We will use a campus bicycle-sharing system as the running example. The operator records hourly rentals at each station and must decide how many bicycles to reposition before the morning rush. A forecast that is numerically accurate on average can still be useless if it misses the peak, leaks future information, or ignores that a prediction made at 6 p.m. has different available data from a prediction made at 6 a.m.

<Callout type="tip">

**Forecast contract.** Before choosing a model, write five lines: the target, the forecast origin, the horizon, the information available at that origin, and the loss caused by an error. These five lines determine what “accurate” means.

</Callout>

## Timeline

Let $y_t$ denote rentals during hour $t$. The subscript is part of the model, not decoration. We must know whether $t$ advances by an hour, a day, or an irregular event. If a sensor records at 08:01, 08:59, and 10:03, the row number is not elapsed time. Resampling to a regular grid changes the data and must be documented.

Suppose the decision is bicycle repositioning at 05:30 for the interval 07:00–09:00. Then the forecast origin is 05:30, the target is the total demand in the two-hour window, and weather forecasts available by 05:30 may be predictors. Realized 07:00 rainfall is not available at the forecast origin and cannot be used during honest backtesting. This is the first and most common time-series error: a column exists in the historical file, so we forget that it did not yet exist when the decision was made.

Three clocks should be kept separate: **event time**, when the rental occurred; **availability time**, when its record became usable; and **decision time**, when the forecast had to be issued. Late reporting can make these differ. A daily hospital census may describe midnight occupancy but become reliable only at 08:00. A financial series may be revised weeks later. A competition report should state which version was available in real time.

A useful first plot contains the raw series, missing intervals, interventions, holidays, and train-validation boundaries. Do not smooth the only copy of the plot. Sudden spikes that look ugly may be the events the decision maker most cares about.

### The smallest baseline

For horizon $h$, the naïve forecast is

$$\hat y_{T+h\mid T}=y_T.$$

For seasonal period $m$, the seasonal naïve forecast repeats the corresponding value from the previous season. For hourly bicycle demand with a weekly rhythm, $m=168$. “Same hour last week” may outperform an impressive model when patterns are stable. That is not embarrassing. A proposed method earns its complexity only by improving a relevant metric on future-like windows.

## Pattern

We usually describe a series using four ideas: level, trend, seasonality, and remainder. An additive representation is

$$y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t,$$

while a multiplicative representation is

$$y_t=T_tS_tR_t.$$

The additive form says seasonal amplitude is roughly constant. The multiplicative form says the seasonal swing grows with the level. If weekday demand oscillates by about 50 rentals whether the station is busy or quiet, additive decomposition is plausible. If the swing is approximately 20% of demand, a log transform or multiplicative model is more natural.

Trend is a long-run movement, not every local rise. Seasonality repeats at a known calendar frequency. A cycle may recur without a fixed period. Remainder is not “meaningless noise”; it contains measurement error, omitted mechanisms, rare events, and structural changes. We inspect it because a model that leaves obvious autocorrelation has not extracted all predictable structure.

For an odd moving-average window $2k+1$,

$$\widehat T_t=\frac{1}{2k+1}\sum_{j=-k}^{k}y_{t+j}.$$

A centered moving average uses future values and is appropriate for retrospective description, not real-time forecasting at the right edge. A trailing average uses only current and past observations, but lags a turning point. This small distinction—centered versus trailing—often separates an attractive analysis from an implementable forecast.

## Dependence

Ordinary regression often assumes residual independence. Time-series data tell us to test rather than assume it. The lag-$k$ sample autocorrelation is

$$r_k=\frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)(y_{t-k}-\bar y)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)^2}.$$

An autocorrelation function (ACF) plots $r_k$ against $k$. A strong $r_{24}$ in hourly data suggests a daily relationship; a strong $r_{168}$ suggests a weekly one. But correlation at many lags can be induced by trend. Two neighboring points are both high simply because the whole series is rising. Detrending or differencing may be necessary before interpreting the ACF as short-run dependence.

First differencing gives

$$\nabla y_t=y_t-y_{t-1},$$

and seasonal differencing gives $\nabla_m y_t=y_t-y_{t-m}$. Differencing can remove a stochastic trend or seasonal level, but it also amplifies high-frequency noise. We should not difference mechanically until every plot looks flat. The question is whether the resulting process has a stable enough mean, variance, and dependence structure for the intended model.

Weak stationarity requires constant mean, constant finite variance, and covariance depending only on lag. Real systems are rarely stationary forever. We seek a window and representation over which stationarity is useful, while explicitly testing interventions and regime changes.

For $y=(10,13,16,19)$, first differences are $(3,3,3)$, revealing linear growth in this tiny sample. A naïve level forecast gives 19; a drift forecast estimates slope

$$\widehat b=\frac{y_T-y_1}{T-1}=3$$

and predicts $\hat y_{5\mid4}=22$. This does not prove the future will rise by three. It proves what assumption the forecast makes. When the next value arrives, the error tests that assumption.

## Smoothing

Simple exponential smoothing updates a latent level:

$$\ell_t=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1},\qquad 0<\alpha<1,$$

and forecasts $\hat y_{t+h\mid t}=\ell_t$. Expanding the recursion shows geometrically decaying weights on past observations. Large $\alpha$ reacts quickly but follows noise; small $\alpha$ is stable but slow after a change.

If $\ell_{t-1}=100$, $y_t=120$, and $\alpha=0.25$, then $\ell_t=105$. The model does not simply “average 100 and 120”; it applies an explicit response speed. That speed should be chosen from forecast performance rather than taste.

Holt's method adds a trend:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_t&=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}),\\
b_t&=\beta(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta)b_{t-1},\\
\hat y_{t+h\mid t}&=\ell_t+hb_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

A damped trend expresses skepticism that growth continues forever. Holt-Winters methods add seasonal states. Their strength is not that they are old or simple; it is that each state has a readable role and can be updated online.

## ARIMA

An autoregressive model of order $p$ writes the present as a linear combination of previous values:

$$y_t=c+\phi_1y_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_py_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t.$$

A moving-average model of order $q$ writes it using previous shocks:

$$y_t=c+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.$$

ARIMA$(p,d,q)$ applies these structures after differencing $d$ times. “Moving average” here means a model of error terms, not the rolling average used for smoothing. Confusing those two meanings produces many bad explanations.

For AR(1), $y_t=c+\phi y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$. If $|\phi|<1$, the stationary mean is $\mu=c/(1-\phi)$. The $h$-step conditional forecast tends toward that mean:

$$\hat y_{T+h\mid T}=\mu+\phi^h(y_T-\mu).$$

When $\phi$ is near one, shocks persist. When $\phi$ is negative, forecasts alternate around the mean. These are behavioral implications, so the fitted coefficient must be interpreted rather than merely reported.

Model identification uses plots, ACF/PACF behavior, information criteria, and residual checks together. A lower AIC does not repair leakage or a wrong horizon. The residual ACF should contain no practically important structure, and residual scale should be stable enough for interval forecasts.

## Validation

Random train-test splitting destroys the forecasting question. A model trained on October should not predict March as though October were already known. Use a chronological holdout or rolling-origin evaluation. At origin $T_i$, fit using observations $1{:}T_i$, predict $T_i+h$, record the error, then advance the origin.

For errors $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$,

$$\operatorname{MAE}=\frac1n\sum_i|e_i|,
\qquad
\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_ie_i^2}.
$$

RMSE penalizes large misses more strongly. MAE is in the target's units and is less dominated by one extreme. MAPE is unstable near zero. MASE scales error by an in-sample naïve benchmark and is useful across series. No metric is universally correct: a bicycle shortage of 20 at 08:00 may cost far more than an excess of 20 at 02:00, so decision-weighted loss may be required.

**Leakage audit.** Fit imputation, scaling, decomposition, feature selection, and hyperparameters inside each training window. Any statistic computed once on the complete timeline can carry future information backward.

</Callout>

## Uncertainty

A point forecast is not a complete forecast. A prediction interval should widen with horizon because more future shocks accumulate. Under a simple independent Gaussian error approximation,

$$\hat y_{T+h\mid T}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\widehat\sigma_h$$

is a nominal $(1-\alpha)$ interval. The hard part is estimating $\sigma_h$ honestly. Residuals may be non-Gaussian, autocorrelated, heteroscedastic, or structurally changed. Bootstrap or simulation intervals can preserve more of that structure when designed carefully.

Coverage is testable. If 95% intervals contain only 72% of held-out observations, they are overconfident even if point RMSE is excellent. Also inspect interval width: trivially wide intervals cover everything but support no decision.

For bicycle repositioning, a station with predicted demand 50 and interval [20,80] should not be treated like one with [47,53]. A robust decision can test several demand quantiles or minimize expected shortage plus a tail-risk penalty.

## Decision

Return to morning repositioning. Let $s_i$ be bicycles placed at station $i$, $\tilde d_i$ uncertain demand, and $u_i=(\tilde d_i-s_i)_+$ unmet demand. A simple decision model is

$$\min_s\;\text{repositioning cost}+\lambda\,\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_i u_i\right],$$

subject to fleet and truck constraints. The forecast is now an input to a decision model. Better RMSE matters only insofar as it improves the decision, especially at constrained stations and peak hours.

Report the baseline, proposed forecast, interval coverage, performance by horizon and station, and resulting operational change. Stress weather shifts, event days, sensor outages, and demand regimes. If the recommendation changes under a modest perturbation, that sensitivity is part of the answer.

The practical workflow is: define origin, horizon, information set, and decision loss; audit timestamps and units; plot raw data and establish naïve baselines; decompose pattern and inspect dependence; fit a small family of interpretable candidates; validate with rolling origins; calibrate uncertainty; and translate forecasts into decisions.

The main lesson is simple: **time order is a constraint**. Respecting it is more important than choosing a fashionable model.

## 时间线

设 $y_t$ 表示第 小时的租赁量。 $t$下标是模型的一部分，而不是装饰。我们必须知道 $t$ 是按小时、按天还是按不规则事件推进的。如果传感器记录时间为08:01、08:59和10:03，则行号不是经过的时间。重新采样到规则网格会改变数据，并且必须记录。

假设决策是在05:30进行自行车重置，用于07:00–09:00的时间段。那么预测起始时间是05:30，目标是这两个小时窗口内的总需求，而在05:30时可获得的天气预报可能是预测变量。实际07:00的降雨量在预测起始时间不可用，并且在真实的回测中不能使用。这是第一个也是最常见的时间序列错误：历史文件中存在一列数据，所以我们忘记了在做决策时它还不存在。

三个时间应当保持分开： **事件时间**，即发生租赁的时间； **可用时间**，当其记录变得可用时；并且 **decision time**，当必须发布预测时。延迟报告可能导致这些有所不同。每日医院统计可能描述午夜时的占用情况，但只有在08:00之后才可靠。财务数据系列可能在几周后才会修订。竞赛报告应说明实时可用的是哪个版本。

一个有用的初步图包含原始序列、缺失区间、干预、假期以及训练-验证边界。不要平滑图的唯一副本。看起来突兀的突然峰值可能正是决策者最关心的事件。

### 最小的基线

对于预测期 $h$，天真预测是

$$\hat y_{T+h\mid T}=y_T.$$

对于季节性周期 $m$，季节性天真预测会重复上一季对应的数值。对于具有每周节奏的每小时自行车需求， $m=168$。“上周同一时间”的预测在模式稳定时可能胜过一个复杂模型。这并不可怕。一个提出的方法只有在提高了未来窗口上的相关指标时，其复杂性才是有价值的。

## 模式

我们通常用四个概念描述序列：水平、趋势、季节性和余项。加法表示为

$$y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t,$$

乘法表示为

$$y_t=T_tS_tR_t.$$

加法形式表示季节幅度大致恒定。乘法形式表示季节波动随水平增长。如果工作日需求无论站点繁忙与否大约 oscillates 50 次租赁，加法分解是合理的。如果波动约为需求的20%，对数变换或乘法模型更自然。

趋势是长期运动，而不是每一次局部上升。季节性以已知的日历频率重复。周期可能没有固定周期地重复。余项并非“无意义的噪声”；它包含测量误差、遗漏的机制、罕见事件和结构性变化。我们检查它是因为一个保留明显自相关的模型尚未提取所有可预测的结构。

对于奇数移动平均窗口 $2k+1$,

$$\widehat T_t=\frac{1}{2k+1}\sum_{j=-k}^{k}y_{t+j}.$$

中心移动平均使用未来值，适用于回顾性描述，而不适合右端的实时预测。滞后平均仅使用当前和过去的观测值，但在转折点上会滞后。这一小小的区别——中心还是滞后——往往决定了分析是否有吸引力，或者能否转化为可实施的预测。

## 依赖性

普通回归常假设残差独立。时间序列数据告诉我们应当检验而非假设它。滞后-$k$ 样本自相关是

$$r_k=\frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)(y_{t-k}-\bar y)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)^2}.$$

自相关函数（ACF）绘制 $r_k$ 对 $k$。强烈的 $r_{24}$ 在每小时数据中暗示每日关系；一个强烈的 $r_{168}$ 暗示每周关系。但在多个滞后期的相关性可能是由趋势引起的。两个相邻的点都高，仅仅是因为整个序列在上升。在解释自相关函数（ACF）为短期依赖性之前，可能需要去趋势或差分。

一阶差分得到

$$\nabla y_t=y_t-y_{t-1},$$

而季节性差分得到 $\nabla_m y_t=y_t-y_{t-m}$。差分可以去除随机趋势或季节性水平，但也会放大高频噪声。在每个图形看起来平稳之前，我们不应机械地进行差分。关键是所得过程的均值、方差和依赖结构是否足够稳定，以适用于预期模型。

弱平稳性要求均值恒定、有限方差恒定，并且协方差仅依赖于滞后。真实系统很少能永远保持平稳。我们寻找一个窗口和表示方法，使平稳性有用，同时明确地测试干预和状态变化。

对于 $y=(10,13,16,19)$，一阶差分为 $(3,3,3)$，在这个微小样本中显示出线性增长。一个天真的水平预测结果是19；漂移预测估计斜率

$$\widehat b=\frac{y_T-y_1}{T-1}=3$$

并进行预测 $\hat y_{5\mid4}=22$. 这并不能证明未来会增加三。它证明了预测所做的假设。当下一个值到达时，误差会检验该假设。

## 平滑

简单指数平滑会更新潜在水平：

$$\ell_t=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1},\qquad 0<\alpha<1,$$

并进行预测 $\hat y_{t+h\mid t}=\ell_t$。展开递归显示对过去观察值的几何衰减权重。大的 $\alpha$ 反应迅速但会跟随噪声；小的 $\alpha$ 稳定但在变化后反应缓慢。

如果 $\ell_{t-1}=100$, $y_t=120$，并且 $\alpha=0.25$，那么 $\ell_t=105$. 该模型并不是简单地“平均100和120”；它应用了一个显式的响应速度。这个速度应该从预测性能中选择，而不是根据个人偏好选择。

霍尔特方法增加了趋势：

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_t&=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}),\\
b_t&=\beta(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta)b_{t-1},\\
\hat y_{t+h\mid t}&=\ell_t+hb_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

阻尼趋势表达了对增长永远持续的怀疑。霍尔特-温特斯方法增加了季节状态。它们的优势不在于它们古老或简单；而在于每个状态都有可读的作用，并且可以在线更新。

## ARIMA

一个阶数为的自回归模型 $p$ 将当前值表示为前期值的线性组合：

$$y_t=c+\phi_1y_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_py_{t-p}+\varepsilon_t.$$

一个阶数为的移动平均模型 $q$ 使用之前的冲击来表示当前值：

$$y_t=c+\varepsilon_t+\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}.$$

ARIMA$(p,d,q)$ 在差分之后应用这些结构 $d$ 时间。“移动平均”在这里指的是误差项的模型，而不是用于平滑的滚动平均。混淆这两种含义会产生很多错误的解释。

对于 AR(1)， $y_t=c+\phi y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$如果 $|\phi|<1$，平稳均值为 $\mu=c/(1-\phi)$。 $h$步条件预测趋向于该均值：

$$\hat y_{T+h\mid T}=\mu+\phi^h(y_T-\mu).$$

当 $\phi$ 接近1时，冲击会持续。当 $\phi$ 为负时，预测围绕均值交替变化。这些都是行为上的含义，因此拟合系数必须被解释，而不仅仅是报告。

模型识别同时使用图形、ACF/PACF 行为、信息准则以及残差检验。较低的 AIC 并不能修正泄露或错误的预测期。残差 ACF 不应包含实际重要的结构，且残差尺度应足够稳定，以便区间预测。

## 验证

随机的训练-测试拆分会破坏预测问题。在十月训练的模型不应该像十月已知一样去预测三月。应使用按时间顺序的保留集或滚动起点评估。在起点 $T_i$，使用观测值 $1{:}T_i$进行拟合，预测 $T_i+h$，记录误差，然后推进起点。

对于误差 $e_i=y_i-\hat y_i$,

$$\operatorname{MAE}=\frac1n\sum_i|e_i|,
\qquad
\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_ie_i^2}.
$$

RMSE 对大的偏差惩罚更严格。MAE 的单位与目标相同，不会被极端值主导。MAPE 在接近零时不稳定。MASE 通过样本内的简单基准缩放误差，对不同序列都适用。没有哪种指标是普遍正确的：在08:00缺少20辆自行车可能造成的损失远超02:00多余20辆，因此可能需要基于决策加权的损失。

![课程资料库中的滚动评估：每个测试块必须严格位于其训练窗口之后。](/mathematical-modeling/timeseries-25.webp)

<Callout type="tip">

**泄漏审计** 在每个训练窗口内进行拟合的插补、缩放、分解、特征选择和超参数调整。任何在完整时间线上计算一次的统计量都可能将未来信息向前泄漏。

</Callout>

## 不确定性

点预测并不是完整的预测。预测区间应随着预测期的延长而扩大，因为更多的未来冲击会积累。在一个简单的独立高斯误差近似下，

$$\hat y_{T+h\mid T}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\widehat\sigma_h$$

是一个名义上的 $(1-\alpha)$ 区间。困难的部分在于诚实地估计 $\sigma_h$ 。残差可能是非高斯的、自相关的、异方差的，或者存在结构性变化。当精心设计时，自助法或模拟区间可以保留更多的这种结构。

覆盖率是可检验的。如果95%的区间中只包含了72%的留出观察值，即使点预测的均方根误差(RMSE)非常好，这些区间也会过度自信。同时检查区间宽度：显然过宽的区间可以覆盖一切，但对决策没有支持作用。

对于自行车重新调配，预测需求为50且区间为[20,80]的车站不应被像[47,53]的车站那样对待。稳健的决策可以测试多个需求分位数，或最小化预期短缺加上尾部风险惩罚。

## 决策

返回到早晨重新调配。设 $s_i$ 为放置在车站的自行车数量 $i$, $\tilde d_i$ 不确定的需求，并且 $u_i=(\tilde d_i-s_i)_+$ 未满足的需求。一个简单的决策模型是

$$\min_s\;\text{repositioning cost}+\lambda\,\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_i u_i\right],$$

受船队和卡车限制约束。预测现在是决策模型的输入。更好的RMSE只在它能改善决策时才重要，特别是在受限站点和高峰时段。

报告基准、提出的预测、区间覆盖率、按预测期和站点划分的性能以及由此产生的操作变化。强调天气变化、活动日、传感器故障和需求状态。如果在适度扰动下建议发生变化，这种敏感性是答案的一部分。

实际工作流程是：定义起点、预测期、信息集和决策损失；审核时间戳和单位；绘制原始数据并建立简单基准；分解模式并检查依赖性；拟合一小组可解释候选模型；用滚动起点进行验证；校准不确定性；并将预测转化为决策。

主要的经验教训很简单： **时间顺序是一种约束**。尊重它比选择时髦的模型更重要。
